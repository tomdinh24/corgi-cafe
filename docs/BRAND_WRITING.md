# Corgi Cafe Brand Writing Guide

- **Status:** Authoritative writing standard for public Cafe and community copy
- **Audience:** Marketing, Product, Design, and anyone writing a public Corgi Cafe experience
- **Last studied:** August 15, 2026

## 1. Scope

This guide governs public writing for Corgi Cafe and Corgi community experiences. It covers landing
pages, events, social posts, product prompts, empty states, trust messages, and calls to action.

It does not replace the product specification. Product documents may use precise terms such as
eligibility, visibility, current intent, and session expiration. Public writing should translate
those rules into language a Cafe visitor understands immediately.

## 2. Source hierarchy

Use Corgi's current public writing as evidence, not as a bank of slogans to copy.

### Primary Corgi references

1. [Corgi Cafe](https://www.corgicafe.com/) for direct, energetic, builder-focused language.
2. [Corgi Cafe residency](https://www.corgicafe.com/residency) for conversational storytelling,
   concrete offers, and playful conviction.
3. [Host with Corgi](https://www.corgicafe.com/host) for clear promises, short calls to action, and
   useful proof.
4. [Corgi events](https://luma.com/usecorgi) for how the brand names real gatherings and activities.

[Corgi Insurance](https://www.corgi.insure/) is a secondary reference for precision, proof, and
confidence. Its institutional and technical language should not be carried into Cafe community
hooks.

### Consumer-social references

The strongest relevant products lead with a human outcome and make the social experience tangible:

- [Timeleft](https://timeleft.com/) promises a path from strangers to friends, then removes planning
  and social uncertainty.
- [Meetup](https://www.meetup.com/) connects shared interests with friendship and quickly shows real
  local activity.
- [222](https://www.222.place/) states the activity and group format instead of explaining its
  matching system.
- [Geneva](https://www.geneva.com/) uses a simple online-to-offline contrast to explain its role.
- [Partiful](https://www.partiful.com/) opens with social energy, then states the practical job it
  makes easier.
- [Bumble BFF](https://bumble.com/bff) speaks to the visitor's life situation and desire to find
  people who understand them.

The repeatable pattern is:

1. Human desire.
2. Concrete social scene.
3. Clear invitation.
4. Simple mechanism.
5. Reassurance in ordinary language.

## 3. Voice north star

> Corgi sounds like a friendly Cafe host who knows the room, gets to the point, and makes the first
> move feel easy.

Corgi is:

- **Clear:** Say what a person can do or experience.
- **Conversational:** Write to one visitor using `you`, `we`, and natural contractions.
- **Concrete:** Name the room, the conversation, the action, or the time.
- **Warm:** Invite people in without pressuring them.
- **Builder-literate:** Understand startup life without performing startup jargon.
- **Playful on purpose:** Use a social observation or a small turn of phrase that advances the idea.
- **Honest:** Make uncertainty and limits easy to understand without leading with caveats.

Corgi is not a networking platform speaking about users. It is a host speaking to a person who is
already in the Cafe.

## 4. The writing formula

Use this sequence for consumer-facing pages:

1. **Invitation:** Name the thing the visitor may want.
2. **Promise:** Explain what Corgi will do in one sentence.
3. **Action:** Give one obvious next step.
4. **Mechanism:** Show the experience in no more than three steps when possible.
5. **Trust:** Answer the worry that could stop the person from acting.

Do not make the visitor understand matching, profiles, permissions, or system states before they
understand the invitation.

### Default landing-page structure

- **Hook:** A question or plain statement a visitor understands in five seconds.
- **Supporting line:** What the visitor tells Corgi and what Corgi does next.
- **CTA:** The next action, not a description of the whole flow.
- **How it works:** Three human steps.
- **Example:** A believable conversation and a natural way to begin it.
- **Ground rules:** Short reassurances about choice, privacy, pitches, and no-match behavior.
- **Final invitation:** Return to the human outcome and repeat the action.

## 5. Hooks

A Corgi hook should contain at least two of these three elements:

- a recognizable moment: sitting at Corgi, working on something, wanting to meet someone;
- a human outcome: a conversation, an introduction, advice, or shared experience;
- a Corgi advantage: the other person may already be in the room and Corgi can make the introduction.

### Preferred hook styles

**Direct invitation**

> Want to meet someone at Corgi?

**Specific social observation**

> Skip the "So, what do you do?"

**Builder recognition**

> Someone here might get what you're building.

Use the direct invitation when clarity matters most. Use a more playful hook only when the next line
makes the experience equally clear.

### Hooks to avoid

- Abstract intrigue: “The right person might already be here.”
- Generic inspiration: “You came to build. Don't build alone.”
- Empty lifestyle language: “Good conversations start here.”
- Product terminology: “Meet a relevant, receptive community member.”
- Transactional judgment: “Find someone worth meeting.”
- Guaranteed outcomes: “Meet your next investor.”

## 6. Playfulness that stays on topic

Playfulness should come from a true part of the social experience:

- bad small talk;
- wondering whether someone wants to be approached;
- working alone in a room full of builders;
- being one table away from useful experience;
- changing your mind without an awkward explanation.

Good playful writing reveals that Corgi understands the moment:

- “No awkward hovering.”
- “Skip the cold approach.”
- “No one to introduce right now. We won't force it.”
- “Changed your mind? Skip it. No explanation needed.”

Avoid random corgi jokes, forced tech references, meme language, puns unrelated to the action, and
multiple jokes in one section. One well-placed wink is enough.

## 7. Calls to action

A CTA should tell the visitor what happens when they select it.

Prefer one to three familiar words:

- Start an intro
- Join the dinner
- Find an event
- Apply now
- Order online

Avoid:

- I'm open to talk
- Unlock my network
- Activate my session
- Explore possibilities
- Begin community onboarding

Do not put a disclaimer, status, or helper sentence beneath a CTA. Put necessary context in the
supporting copy, a trust section, a form field, or a status message where it belongs.

## 8. Translate product language

| Internal language | Public language |
|---|---|
| Relevant and receptive | Someone you may want to talk to who is open to meeting |
| Mutually eligible | You both said yes to this kind of conversation |
| Current intent | What you want to talk about today |
| Offer | What you can share or help with |
| Interaction boundaries | Conversations you are and are not up for |
| Active introduction session | Open to an introduction while you are here |
| Session expiration | Only while you are here, or until the time you choose |
| Strong mutual fit | A good reason for you two to talk |
| No eligible match | No one to introduce right now |
| Simultaneous recommendation | Corgi introduces you both at the same time |
| Visibility | Who can see that you are open to meeting |

Keep legally or operationally necessary terms in supporting disclosures. Do not use them as hooks,
headlines, buttons, or friendly reassurance.

## 9. Writing by surface

### Landing pages

Lead with the invitation. Keep the first explanation to one or two short sentences. Show a real
example before listing product rules.

**Do**

> Want to meet someone at Corgi?
>
> Tell us what you're working on and what you'd like to talk about. If someone here wants that
> conversation too, we'll introduce you.

**Do not**

> Corgi helps connection-seeking visitors discover relevant, receptive community members through
> mutually compatible, time-bounded sessions.

### Social posts

Start with the moment, the person, or the activity. Include the concrete details someone needs to
decide whether to care. End with one action.

> Building late? Come work from Corgi. We're open all night at 9 Claude Lane.

### Events

Name the activity before describing the community benefit.

> Bring your demo. Get honest feedback. Meet other people shipping this week.

### Trust and permission

State the reassuring behavior, not the policy architecture.

- “You only meet when you're both up for it.”
- “No surprise pitches.”
- “Your profile is not a public directory listing.”
- “Skip whenever you want. We won't say who skipped.”

### Empty states

Say what happened and what the visitor can do next. Do not make a correct no-match result sound like
an error.

> No one to introduce right now. Stay open while you're here or try again next time.

### Product prompts

Ask one ordinary question at a time:

- “What are you working on today?”
- “What would you like to talk about?”
- “What could you help someone else with?”
- “Any conversations you're not up for?”

## 10. Avoid list

Remove or rewrite:

- abstract SaaS language such as activate, optimize, facilitate, leverage, and ecosystem;
- product-spec language used as marketing, including relevant, receptive, eligible, intent, and
  session;
- stacked qualifiers that make a simple promise hard to understand;
- networking language such as high-value connections, unlock your network, and curated access;
- hype about investors, jobs, customers, funding, exclusivity, or guaranteed outcomes;
- sentences beginning with “Corgi can help you” when Corgi can simply state what it does;
- helper-copy phrasing such as “This allows you to” or “in order to”;
- em dashes in public UI copy;
- a joke that does not clarify the product, ease tension, or move the visitor forward.

## 11. Editing checklist

Read every public line aloud, then confirm:

- Can a first-time visitor understand the offer in five seconds?
- Does the opening name a human desire rather than a product capability?
- Does each sentence contain one idea?
- Are the nouns concrete and the verbs familiar?
- Could a Cafe host say this naturally to one person?
- Does the playful line come from a real part of the experience?
- Is the action obvious without helper text beneath it?
- Are uncertainty, privacy, and permission stated plainly?
- Does the copy avoid ranking a person's worth?
- Does every claim stay inside the accepted product boundary?

If any answer is no, rewrite before design begins.
