# Corgi Cafe Community Connection
## Product Context & MVP Brief for Codex

### Purpose of this document

Use this document as the product and strategic context for designing or prototyping improvements to the **Corgi Cafe app onboarding and activation experience**, specifically around helping members connect with valuable people in the Corgi community.

The immediate product problem is:

> **How might Corgi help a new Cafe member quickly understand who they should talk to, why they should talk to them, and make that first interaction happen?**

The product should optimize for **real-world human connection inside Corgi**, not engagement with the app itself.

---

# 1. Corgi strategic context

Corgi should not be understood simply as an insurance startup that happens to operate cafes.

The broader company philosophy appears to be:

1. Vertically integrate important parts of the business.
2. Own and operate infrastructure rather than depending entirely on intermediaries.
3. Take contrarian bets when complexity can become a competitive advantage.
4. Move quickly.
5. Run experiments.
6. Kill things that do not work.
7. Aggressively double down on things that do.
8. Build a distinctive brand around an otherwise low-frequency and traditionally boring industry.

Corgi Cafe fits into this philosophy.

The Head of Community describes the Cafe as a **“third space for builders across tech.”**

The target community includes people such as:

- Founders
- Engineers
- Operators
- Investors
- Early adopters
- AI builders
- Startup employees
- Venture-backed founders
- Venture funds
- Ecosystem partners
- Artists and creatives
- Eventually broader small-business communities

The cafe is intentionally open 24/7 because builders may work late at night or early in the morning.

Corgi does not view the Cafe only through direct cafe unit economics or direct insurance conversion.

The Cafe can create:

- Community
- Brand affinity
- Customer acquisition
- Partnerships
- Content
- Recruiting
- Founder relationships
- Investor relationships
- Ecosystem density
- Familiarity and trust with Corgi
- A positive brand halo around the insurance business

The cafe can therefore be considered a **physical frontend to the larger Corgi ecosystem**.

# 2. Why community matters to Corgi

Traditional insurance has a frequency problem.

Someone may interact with their insurer only when:

- purchasing a policy,
- renewing,
- signing an enterprise contract,
- raising money,
- dealing with a compliance requirement,
- or filing a claim.

That makes it difficult to build a frequent relationship.

Corgi Cafe changes this.

Someone may interact with Corgi through:

- coffee,
- coworking,
- events,
- dinners,
- hackathons,
- workshops,
- founder gatherings,
- partner activations,
- investor meetings,
- social activities,
- community introductions,
- content,
- and repeated visits.

Corgi can therefore build a relationship with someone **before that person has an immediate insurance need**.

The deeper strategic opportunity is not:

> Build community so we can advertise insurance to people.

It is closer to:

> Become a valuable institution within the founder and builder ecosystem, so Corgi naturally sits at the center of relationships, information, opportunities, and eventually financial services.

Community is therefore both:

### Customer value

Corgi helps builders:

- meet collaborators,
- find employees,
- raise capital,
- find customers,
- get advice,
- form partnerships,
- discover opportunities,
- find belonging,
- and meet interesting people.

### Strategic value for Corgi

Those interactions create:

- recurring engagement,
- trust,
- brand affinity,
- customer acquisition,
- partnerships,
- talent acquisition,
- proprietary community density,
- and a reason for people to repeatedly interact with Corgi.

# 3. How Corgi currently creates community

Corgi already uses several mechanisms.

## 3.1 Physical third space

The cafe provides a permanent location rather than an occasional event.

This matters because traditional community events are episodic:

> Event → people meet → everyone leaves → relationship disappears.

A permanent space allows:

> Event → cowork → return tomorrow → encounter someone again → attend another event → relationship develops.

This produces repeated collisions.

---

## 3.2 Events and programming

Corgi uses activities to give people a reason to gather.

Examples discussed in our research include:

- Founder dinners
- Demo nights
- Pitch events
- Hackathons
- Build nights
- Coworking
- Coffee meetups
- Workshops
- Partner events
- Startup gatherings
- Social activities
- Sports
- Community dinners
- Artist programs

The important insight is:

> **Networking does not have to be the stated activity.**

“Come network” creates social friction.

“Come build something,” “come have dinner,” “come play,” or “come learn something” gives people a shared activity from which relationships can naturally emerge.

# 4. Corgi's community operating model

The Head of Community podcast adds several useful principles.

## 4.1 Corgi prefers owned community infrastructure

Corgi experimented with temporary “Corgi House” activations around conferences in different cities.

Those were:

- expensive,
- operationally intensive,
- difficult to build community around quickly,
- and hard to sustain.

The team stopped doing them.

Instead, Corgi doubled down on its permanent Cafe and now prefers bringing community activity into spaces Corgi owns and operates.

This aligns with the broader Corgi bias toward vertical integration.

### Product implication

The app should make **Corgi-owned physical spaces more valuable**, rather than becoming a generalized networking app detached from Corgi.

---

## 4.2 Content is the top of the community funnel

Corgi's Head of Community describes content as a pipeline for:

- community,
- hiring,
- insurance sales,
- partnerships,
- cafe brand partnerships,
- and expansion into new cities.

The model roughly looks like:

**Content**

→ awareness

→ community interest

→ cafe/event participation

→ relationships

→ partnerships/customers/hiring/community

### Product implication

The app begins further down this funnel.

A member has already expressed meaningful intent by:

- entering the Cafe,
- attending an event,
- joining the Corgi community,
- or downloading the app.

The app's responsibility is therefore not primarily awareness.

It should convert **physical/community presence into useful relationships**.

# 5. Core product insight

Corgi Cafe may already contain a high concentration of interesting people.

The problem is that this network is largely invisible.

A new person walks into the Cafe and experiences:

> “There are probably 40 interesting people around me, but I don't know who they are, what they're working on, what they need, whether they can help me, or whether they even want to talk.”

This creates a **network legibility problem**.

The network exists.

The user cannot understand it.

The core product transformation should therefore be:

> **Room full of strangers → 3 people I have a reason to meet.**

---

# 6. The professional community cold-start problem

Professional communities repeatedly suffer from three problems:

### 1. Who should I talk to?

There may be hundreds or thousands of members.

The user cannot evaluate everyone.

### 2. Why should I talk to them?

Even if someone looks interesting, relevance is unclear.

A profile containing:

> Founder @ Acme AI

does not explain why meeting that person is valuable.

### 3. How do we sustain the relationship?

After the initial meeting, communities struggle to create repeated meaningful interactions.

For the current Corgi onboarding and activation problem, focus primarily on:

**1. Who?**

and

**2. Why?**

Long-term retention and relationship maintenance can come later.

---

# 7. Product thesis

> **Corgi doesn't need another LinkedIn. It needs to make the people already inside Corgi legible and approachable.**

LinkedIn optimizes around:

> Who exists in the professional world?

Corgi can optimize around:

> **Who should I meet here, right now?**

Corgi has several contextual advantages that traditional professional networks lack:

- Physical proximity
- Shared location
- Shared event
- Current availability
- Community membership
- Repeated physical encounters
- Human community operators
- A clear reason for being present

This makes Corgi fundamentally different from a generic professional social network.

# 8. Target user

The initial user should be someone who is physically participating in the Corgi ecosystem.

Examples:

- Founder working from Corgi Cafe
- Founder attending a Corgi event
- Investor visiting the Cafe
- Operator looking to meet founders
- Engineer exploring startup opportunities
- Builder new to San Francisco
- Repeat Cafe regular
- Community/event participant

For the first version, founders are likely the strongest starting segment because their networking goals are concrete and relatively easy to model.

# 9. Founder intent model

A founder may enter Corgi because they want to:

### Professional intents

- Fundraise
- Hire
- Find customers
- Find partnerships
- Ask for advice
- Find expertise
- Meet investors
- Meet other founders
- Find collaborators

### Social / exploratory intents

- Meet interesting people
- Meet people with similar interests
- Learn what others are building
- Make friends in tech
- Explore without a specific transactional goal

Not every interaction should be transactional.

The system should support both:

> “I need a seed investor.”

and

> “I just want to meet interesting AI builders.”

# 10. Separate intent from openness

These are different concepts.

## Intent

**What am I trying to accomplish?**

Examples:

- Raising a seed round
- Hiring an engineer
- Looking for design partners
- Finding customers
- Want advice about enterprise sales

## Openness

**What interactions am I willing to have?**

Examples:

- Open to talking to anyone
- Open to founder conversations
- Happy to advise people
- Open to coffee right now
- Open only to highly relevant introductions
- Not looking to network today

This distinction matters because relevance alone does not remove the social uncertainty of approaching someone.

The product must answer:

> **Is this person actually open to being approached?**

# 12. Ask ↔ Offer matching

This should be one of the core matching primitives.

Example:

### Tom

**Ask**
- Fundraising advice
- Seed investor introductions

**Offers**
- AI product
- GTM
- Consumer growth

### Sarah

**Ask**
- Meet AI founders

**Offers**
- Seed fundraising advice
- Early-stage investing

The application can infer:

> Tom is raising and Sarah explicitly offers fundraising advice.

This creates a much stronger reason to connect than:

> You both work in AI.

# 13. Product promise

The onboarding experience should communicate a very simple value proposition:

> **Tell Corgi what you're building and what you need. We'll show you the people here worth meeting.**

The product is not:

- a social feed,
- an infinite directory,
- another LinkedIn,
- an event discovery app,
- or a professional dating app.

The product is:

> **A contextual community concierge.**

---

# 14. Onboarding principle

Only collect enough information to create the **first useful connection**.

Avoid:

> Create account → 15 profile fields → biography → full resume → interests → preferences → permissions → finally enter product.

Corgi should produce value quickly.

Target:

> **Onboarding → recommendations in under ~60 seconds.**

Exact timing should be validated rather than treated as a hard requirement.

# 16. Activation experience

Immediately after onboarding, show:

# People worth meeting at Corgi right now

Limit the initial experience to roughly **3 recommendations**.

Do not show an endless directory.

Every recommendation must clearly answer:

### Who?

Who is this person?

### Why?

Why is this person relevant to me?

### Why now?

Why is meeting them useful in the current context?

### Are they open?

Is approaching them socially appropriate?

### What should I do?

Provide one simple next action.

# 18. Explanation quality is core product functionality

A recommendation without explanation is weak.

Bad:

> “You may want to meet Sarah.”

Better:

> “You both work in AI.”

Strong:

> “You're currently raising a seed round. Sarah raised hers four months ago and has explicitly offered to help first-time founders with fundraising.”

The **why** creates confidence.

It also gives the user their first conversation starter.

Instead of:

> “Hi, what do you do?”

the user can begin with:

> “Corgi mentioned you've recently raised. I'm going through that right now.”

This reduces social friction.

---

# 19. Matching model

The first matching system does not need to be extremely sophisticated.

Start with understandable rules.

Potential signals:

1. Ask ↔ Offer fit
2. Current intent compatibility
3. Physical presence
4. Openness
5. Shared event/context
6. Industry relevance
7. Startup stage
8. Shared interests
9. Semantic relevance between what people are building
10. Previous interactions

Conceptually:

`match_score = ask_offer_fit + intent_fit + presence + openness + shared_context + semantic_relevance`

Weights should be configurable.

Do not prematurely optimize the exact scoring algorithm.

The important product question is:

> **Does the recommendation result in a useful conversation?**

---

# 20. AI's role

AI should improve the product without becoming the product.

Good uses:

### Structured extraction

Convert:

> “I'm building an agent that automates insurance claims.”

into structured concepts such as:

- AI
- Insurance
- Claims
- B2B

### Semantic matching

Understand that:

> “Looking for enterprise design partners”

may match someone who says:

> “I manage claims operations at an insurance carrier.”

even without identical keywords.

### Recommendation explanation

Generate:

> “You should meet Priya because you're looking for claims design partners and she runs claims operations at an insurance company.”

### Ranking

Use semantic relevance as one signal among others.

Avoid positioning this as:

> “AI networking assistant.”

The user does not care how sophisticated the model is.

They care whether they met someone useful.

# 21. Corgi-specific advantage: real-time physical context

Most networking platforms know:

> Someone exists.

Corgi can know:

> Someone relevant is in the same physical community right now and has explicitly indicated that they are open to meeting people.

This changes the product from:

**network discovery**

to:

**network activation**.

That should be the strategic emphasis.

# 22. Check-in

The system needs a lightweight representation of physical presence.

Possible V1:

**I'm at Corgi Cafe**

User checks in manually.

Alternative triggers later:

- Cafe QR code
- Event QR code
- Cafe Wi-Fi
- Event RSVP
- NFC
- Location permission

Avoid background location tracking for V1.

Presence should expire automatically after a reasonable duration.

Example:

> Checked into Corgi SF until 5:00 PM.

Users should always understand when their presence is visible.

---

# 23. Human-in-the-loop community ops

Corgi has something LinkedIn does not:

**a real community team operating inside the physical space.**

Use it.

The first version does not need to fully automate introductions.

Create a lightweight internal community console that shows:

- People currently checked in
- Their asks
- Their offers
- Their openness
- Suggested matches
- Intro requests
- People who have not received matches
- High-value unanswered asks
- Event attendees
- Connection outcomes

A community operator can then say:

> “Tom, you should meet Sarah. She's over there and is happy to talk fundraising.”

This can significantly increase the probability of real-world connection.

It also allows Corgi to learn what makes a strong match before trying to automate everything.

---

# 24. Why the human layer matters for the MVP

Professional-network matching quality is difficult to infer from profile data alone.

Community operators have contextual knowledge such as:

- “Sarah is extremely helpful.”
- “Those two people already know each other.”
- “Alex is busy right now.”
- “Priya specifically asked me to introduce her to AI founders.”
- “These two companies could potentially partner.”
- “This person is new to SF and wants friends.”

Human curation can bootstrap the recommendation system.

Over time:

**human judgment → labeled outcomes → better product rules/models.**

# 25. Cold-start strategy

The product itself has a cold-start problem.

If five people complete profiles but everyone else in the Cafe is invisible, matching will fail.

Possible bootstrap strategy:

## Seed regulars

Invite high-frequency Cafe members to create profiles first.

## Seed community connectors

Founders, investors, operators, and regulars who are naturally open to helping others.

## Event onboarding

Ask attendees to complete lightweight profiles during RSVP or check-in.

## Community staff onboarding

Community team members can personally invite high-value members.

## QR onboarding

Place a clear CTA inside the physical Cafe:

> **Meet people worth knowing at Corgi**

Scan → onboarding → recommendations.

# 26. Activation metric

Avoid treating:

- account creation,
- profile completion,
- app downloads,
- or recommendation views

as the primary activation metric.

Those represent product usage.

The product promise is **connection**.

## Primary activation metric

> **% of newly onboarded members who initiate or accept a relevant connection during their first Corgi visit.**

Possible qualifying events:

- Say hi
- Intro requested
- Intro accepted
- Message sent
- Contact exchanged

---

# 27. Quality activation

Eventually move beyond connection initiation.

A better long-term metric is:

> **% of new members who have at least one mutually acknowledged meaningful conversation during their first visit.**

After the interaction:

### Did you meet?

- Yes
- Not yet

If yes:

### Was this connection useful?

- 👍 Yes
- Neutral
- 👎 No

Potential additional tags:

- Helpful advice
- Potential customer
- Investor connection
- Hiring
- Partnership
- New friend
- Follow-up planned

This becomes both a success metric and training signal.

# 31. Privacy and social safety

Core principles:

### Explicit presence

Only show someone as present if they deliberately check in.

### Explicit openness

Being physically inside Corgi does not automatically mean:

> “Please approach me.”

### No precise physical tracking

Do not show exact seat/location.

### Easy visibility controls

Members should be able to become invisible immediately.

### Easy blocking/reporting

Provide simple controls.

### Mutual relevance

Avoid indiscriminate cold outreach.

### Minimize spam

Do not allow someone to blast every person currently in the Cafe.

# 36. Matching V1

Start deterministic.

Pseudo-logic:

```text
candidate must:
- currently be checked in OR be part of the same active event
- be visible
- have openness != "not open"

score candidate on:
1. source asks ↔ target offers
2. target asks ↔ source offers
3. shared intent
4. shared industry/interests
5. shared event/context
6. semantic similarity between profiles
7. novelty / not previously connected
```

Return:

```text
Top 3 candidates
+
human-readable match explanation
```

Do not optimize weights prematurely.

# 40. Relationship to Key experience

This product problem strongly overlaps with previous work at Key AI.

The transferable insight is **not simply that Key also recommended people.**

The deeper learning is:

> Showing someone a relevant profile is insufficient.

Professional networking breaks when users do not understand:

1. why the other person is relevant,
2. whether the other person wants to interact,
3. or what they should talk about.

At Key, recommendation systems could surface potentially valuable people.

Corgi offers additional signals unavailable to most digital professional networks:

- Physical presence
- Shared space
- Shared event
- Explicit openness
- Repeated encounters
- Community staff who can facilitate introductions

The product opportunity is therefore to take what worked in digital professional recommendations and combine it with **real-world context**.

> **Corgi has already solved one of the hardest problems in professional networking: getting a dense group of interesting people into the same place. The app's job is to make that density legible and actionable.**

Then:

> “A new visitor shouldn't walk into a room with 40 strangers. They should walk into Corgi knowing the three people worth meeting and why.”

---

# 42. Why this sits naturally within Corgi's broader strategy

Corgi's community strategy already creates:

**Place**

→ Cafe

**Programming**

→ Events and experiences

**Identity**

→ Founder, builder, investor, artist, partner communities

**Network**

→ Relationships

The app can become the technology layer connecting those pieces.

Without software:

> Interesting people happen to collide.

With software:

> Corgi can intentionally create high-value collisions.

That means Corgi moves from:

**hosting community**

toward:

**orchestrating community.**

# 43. Longer-term opportunity

Do not build this in V1, but the natural roadmap is:

## Phase 1 — First connection

Who should I meet?

Why?

---

## Phase 2 — Contextual concierge

Who should I meet:

- at this event,
- today,
- this week,
- for this particular problem?

---

## Phase 3 — Relationship memory

Remember:

- who I met,
- what we discussed,
- whether the interaction was useful,
- whether we should reconnect.

---

## Phase 4 — Community graph

Corgi understands:

- what members need,
- what they can offer,
- who has helped whom,
- what communities are forming,
- what opportunities exist.

---

## Phase 5 — Network orchestration

Corgi can proactively say:

> “You should meet Maya because she just started working on the exact problem you were asking about last month.”

At this point, the value is no longer merely:

> “Corgi has interesting people.”

It becomes:

> **“Corgi knows who I should know.”**

That is a much stronger community moat.

---

# 44. Core experiment

The MVP should answer one question:

> **Can Corgi increase the probability that a new Cafe visitor has one valuable conversation during their first visit?**

Test:

### Control

Existing Cafe experience.

### Treatment

Intent onboarding + 3 contextual recommendations + connection CTA.

Measure:

- connection initiation,
- actual conversations,
- perceived usefulness,
- willingness to use it again.

Qualitative interviews matter heavily at this stage.

Ask:

> Why did you choose this person?

> Why did you ignore the others?

> Did knowing they were open make approaching them easier?

> Did the explanation make the recommendation feel credible?

> Would this make you more likely to return to Corgi?

---

# 45. Core hypothesis

> **If we understand what someone wants, what they can offer, and who is physically available around them, then Corgi can reduce the uncertainty of professional networking enough to create more valuable real-world connections.**

# 47. North-star vision

Corgi Cafe should not feel like:

> a coffee shop full of strangers.

It should feel like:

> **a room where Corgi already knows who you should meet.**

The software disappears into the experience.

The ultimate success state is not:

> “I spent 20 minutes using the Corgi app.”

It is:

> **“I walked into Corgi and met someone I never would have met otherwise.”**
